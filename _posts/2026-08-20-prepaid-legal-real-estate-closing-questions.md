## Key Takeaways

- The real value of legal help during a real estate closing tends to come before the closing date, reviewing the purchase agreement and disclosures.
- Some states legally require a real estate attorney to be present at closing - plan coverage supplements this, it doesn't replace a state requirement.
- Residential loan document preparation is a commonly included benefit that's directly relevant to a home purchase.

Buying a home involves signing a stack of documents most buyers have never seen before, under real time pressure to close by a specific date. As covered in the [comparison guide to whether prepaid legal is worth it](https://jredmonson.github.io/law/is-prepaid-legal-worth-it-comparison/), a real estate closing is a good example of a common, recurring-enough legal need that fits well within standard plan coverage.

## Where Legal Help Matters Most in a Closing

The highest-value moment for legal input isn't at the closing table - it's in the days or weeks before, when the purchase agreement, seller disclosures, and financing documents can still be questioned or negotiated. Once at the closing table, most documents are effectively final. A member who reviews the purchase agreement with their provider firm before signing is in a meaningfully better position than one who's seeing every clause for the first time at closing.

## What's Typically Covered

Residential loan document preparation and review of standard real estate paperwork are commonly included benefits, along with general consultations for questions about a pending purchase or sale. This covers the most common need - understanding what's actually in a contract before signing it - without requiring a separate hourly-billed attorney relationship just to ask a handful of questions about a purchase agreement.

## Where State Requirements Come Into Play

A number of states legally require a licensed real estate attorney to be involved in residential closings, independent of any legal plan membership. In those states, plan coverage works alongside that requirement rather than replacing it - the plan can help with questions and document review leading up to closing, while the state-required attorney handles the closing itself. Knowing your state's specific requirement before closing day avoids a last-minute scramble.

## Getting the Most Value Before Your Closing Date

The single most useful thing a buyer can do with plan coverage is get the purchase agreement and disclosure documents reviewed as early as possible after an offer is accepted, not in the final days before closing when there's little room left to negotiate or address a concerning clause. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does plan coverage replace a state-required closing attorney?**
No - in states that require a real estate attorney at closing, that requirement stands independent of plan coverage, which works alongside it rather than replacing it.

**Can a provider firm review a purchase agreement before I sign an offer?**
Generally yes for consultation-level review - this is one of the higher-value uses of coverage, since it happens while terms can still be questioned or negotiated.

**Is seller-side representation covered the same way as buyer-side?**
Coverage generally applies to the member regardless of which side of the transaction they're on, though it's worth confirming specifics for your situation.
