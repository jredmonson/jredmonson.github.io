## What an Estate Planning Attorney in Georgetown Actually Does

An estate planning attorney's job is to translate your wishes into documents that hold up in a Texas probate court, and to structure your assets so your family isn't left guessing or fighting over what you intended. That typically means drafting a will, setting up a power of attorney for finances, a medical power of attorney, and often a revocable living trust if your goal is to avoid probate entirely.

In practice, most engagements start with an intake conversation about your family situation - are you married, do you have minor children, do you own a business, do you have property in more than one county - because Texas estate law treats each of those situations differently. A Georgetown attorney who works regularly in Williamson County courts will also know which local judges and clerks tend to move faster or slower on probate filings, which matters if your family ever needs to use these documents.

## Estate Planning vs. a DIY Will Kit: What Actually Holds Up

A DIY will kit can be legally valid in Texas if it's signed and witnessed correctly, but it commonly fails to address the situations that cause the most family conflict - blended families, business ownership, out-of-state property, or a beneficiary with special needs. An attorney-drafted plan is built to anticipate those specific complications before they become a court fight.

The risk with template documents isn't that they're automatically invalid - it's that generic language doesn't map cleanly onto a specific family's situation. A will that doesn't clearly address a second marriage, a child from a prior relationship, or a jointly-owned business can create exactly the ambiguity that ends up in probate litigation. According to estate planning industry surveys, roughly one in three U.S. adults doesn't have any estate plan at all, and a meaningful share of those who do are relying on outdated or generic documents that haven't been reviewed in years.

## Common Estate Planning Mistakes Texas Families Make

The most common mistake isn't having the wrong documents - it's not updating the documents you have after a major life change. A will drafted before a divorce, a second marriage, the birth of a child, or a significant change in assets can create outcomes nobody intended, because Texas law generally follows what the document says, not what you meant.

A second common mistake is assuming a will avoids probate. In Texas, a will still typically has to go through some form of probate process before assets are distributed - it's a revocable living trust, not a will, that's structured specifically to bypass probate. Families who want to minimize court involvement for their heirs usually need both documents working together, not a will alone.

A third mistake, especially relevant for a retirement-heavy community like Georgetown, is leaving beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance out of sync with the will. Those designations generally override what a will says, so an outdated beneficiary form can undo years of otherwise careful planning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do I need a lawyer to write a will in Texas?**
**A:** No, Texas law allows a self-prepared will if it's properly signed and witnessed, but an attorney-drafted or attorney-reviewed will is far less likely to create ambiguity that leads to a probate dispute, especially for blended families or business owners.

**Q: How much does an estate planning attorney cost in Georgetown, TX?**
**A:** A basic estate plan with a traditional attorney in the Georgetown/Williamson County market typically runs $1,000-$3,000, with more complex trust-based plans costing more; a prepaid legal membership can cover core document preparation for a flat monthly fee instead.

**Q: Does a will avoid probate in Texas?**
**A:** Generally, no - a will still goes through some form of the Texas probate process before assets are distributed. A revocable living trust is the tool typically used to minimize or avoid probate, and many complete plans use a will and a trust together.

**Q: What's the difference between a will and a power of attorney?**
**A:** A will controls what happens to your assets after you pass away, while a power of attorney authorizes someone to act on your behalf - financially or medically - while you're alive but unable to make decisions yourself. A complete plan includes both.
