## What Does a Probate Attorney in Georgetown, TX Actually Do?

A probate attorney in Georgetown handles the court filings needed to open an estate, guides the executor through their legal duties, and resolves any disputes among heirs or creditors - work that ranges from a single filing for a simple estate to months of active case management for a contested one.

The core tasks a probate attorney typically handles:

| Task | What It Involves | When It's Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Filing the application | Petition to probate the will (or open administration if no will) | Every probate case |
| Proving the will | Presenting witnesses or a self-proving affidavit to the court | Every case with a will |
| Qualifying the executor | Oath and, if required, a bond | Every case where an executor is appointed |
| Notice to creditors | Published notice plus direct notice to known creditors | Every administration |
| Inventory or affidavit in lieu | Listing estate assets for the court or beneficiaries | Every administration |
| Resolving disputes | Contested wills, heir disagreements, creditor claims | As-needed |

Most Georgetown families need the first five of these and nothing more. It's the sixth category - disputes - that turns a routine six-month process into something that drags on and costs significantly more.

## How Does Probate Work in Williamson County?

Probate in Williamson County follows Texas's independent administration model for the large majority of cases, meaning the court appoints an executor and then steps back, letting that executor manage the estate without seeking judicial approval for each individual action. This is different from dependent administration, where the court supervises and must approve nearly every decision.

The two paths compared:

- **Independent administration** - Used when the will authorizes it (most wills drafted by an attorney do) or all heirs agree to it. The executor gets "Letters Testamentary" and can then sell property, pay debts, and distribute assets without returning to court for permission each time. This is faster, cheaper, and the default path most Georgetown families end up on.
- **Dependent administration** - Used when there's no will, the will doesn't authorize independent administration, and heirs can't agree to request it, or the estate is contested. The court must approve major actions individually, which adds time and legal fees at every step.

If your family member died with a properly drafted will naming an independent executor, you're very likely headed for the faster path. If there's no will, or the family disagrees about who should serve, expect a longer and more expensive process.

## How Much Does Probate Cost in Georgetown, TX?

Probate costs in Georgetown for a straightforward, uncontested estate typically run $1,500-$4,500 in attorney fees as a flat rate, or $250-$500+ per hour if billed hourly, plus court filing fees that generally run a few hundred dollars. Contested probate, where heirs or creditors dispute the will or the executor's actions, can run into the tens of thousands because it shifts from a handful of filings to ongoing litigation.

What typically drives the total cost:

- **Complexity of the estate** - A single house and a bank account is simple; a business, out-of-state property, or multiple beneficiaries with competing interests is not.
- **Whether the will is contested** - This is the single biggest cost multiplier in probate, full stop.
- **Independent vs. dependent administration** - Dependent administration requires more attorney time because every step needs a court filing and approval.
- **Executor compensation** - Texas law allows an executor a statutory fee (a percentage of amounts received and paid out) unless the will says otherwise; family members often waive this, but it's a cost to plan for if they don't.

The number that surprises people most isn't the attorney fee - it's how much of the total timeline is dictated by mandatory waiting periods (like the creditor notice window) rather than anything an attorney or executor can speed up.

## Do You Always Need Full Probate Administration?

No - Texas offers two shortcuts for qualifying estates: a muniment of title, which transfers real property without appointing an executor at all, and a small estate affidavit, which works for smaller estates with no will. Both are significantly faster and cheaper than full administration when the estate qualifies.

When these shortcuts typically apply:

- **Muniment of title** - Available when there's a valid will, no unpaid debts other than a mortgage secured by real estate, and the primary goal is transferring title to property (usually a home). No executor is appointed, which skips the creditor notice and inventory steps entirely.
- **Small estate affidavit** - Available when there's no will and the estate falls under a statutory value cap (excluding the homestead and certain exempt property) that Texas adjusts periodically. This process skips full administration but still requires a sworn affidavit filed with the court.

Neither shortcut works if there's a dispute among heirs, unresolved debt beyond what's allowed, or real property in more than one state. When they do apply, the difference in cost and timeline is significant enough that it's worth having someone check eligibility before assuming full administration is required.

## When Should You Hire a Probate Attorney vs. Handle It Yourself?

You should hire a probate attorney whenever the estate involves real property, more than a small number of heirs, any disagreement about the will or its interpretation, or debts that exceed the estate's liquid assets - situations where a mistake is expensive and hard to reverse. Texas doesn't legally require an attorney for every probate filing, but courts and clerks expect filings to meet exact legal standards, and a rejected filing costs more time than it saves.

A simple filter for Georgetown families:

- **Consider handling it without an attorney only if:** the estate clearly qualifies for a muniment of title or small estate affidavit, all heirs agree, and there's no real dispute or complexity.
- **Get an attorney involved if:** there's no will, heirs disagree about anything, the estate includes a business or property in multiple counties or states, or a creditor is making an aggressive claim.
- **Always use an attorney for:** any contested will, any dependent administration, or any situation where a family member is threatening litigation.

The mistake families make most often isn't hiring an attorney when they didn't need one - it's the reverse: trying to self-file a straightforward-looking case that turns out to have a complication (an old debt, a missing heir, an out-of-state asset) that a first-time filer has no way to anticipate.

## FAQ

**Q: How long does probate take in Georgetown, TX?**
A: A straightforward, uncontested independent administration typically takes six months to a year to fully close, largely because of the mandatory creditor notice period and inventory deadlines rather than anything the attorney or executor can speed up. Transferring title to real property specifically can often happen sooner than the full estate closes.

**Q: Do all estates in Texas have to go through probate court?**
A: No. Estates that qualify for a muniment of title (a valid will, no debts other than a mortgage) or a small estate affidavit (no will, estate value under a statutory cap) can skip full administration entirely. Whether an estate qualifies depends on its specific assets and debts, which is worth confirming before assuming full probate is required.

**Q: Can a prepaid legal plan handle my entire probate case?**
A: No. A prepaid legal plan like LegalShield can cover consultations and document review for routine probate questions, but it's not built for full case representation in a contested or complex administration. It's most useful for the early "what do we actually need to do here" questions before a family commits to a path.

**Q: What happens if there's no will?**
A: Without a will, the estate is distributed according to Texas intestate succession laws, and the court determines heirs through a formal heirship proceeding. This is typically more complex and expensive than probating a valid will, and it often pushes an estate toward dependent administration if the heirs can't agree on an independent administrator.
