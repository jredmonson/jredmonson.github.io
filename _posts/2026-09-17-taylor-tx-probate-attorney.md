Taylor occupies an interesting spot on the map - close enough to Georgetown and Round Rock to share the same general Williamson County legal infrastructure, but far enough out, and close enough to the county line, that some families here end up dealing with property or relatives split across two counties. That geography doesn't change the underlying Texas probate law, but it does mean a Taylor probate case occasionally needs an extra filing step that a case entirely within one county wouldn't.

The core process - determine whether a valid will exists, qualify an executor, notify creditors, inventory assets, distribute what's left - is identical to what's described for Georgetown generally. What Taylor families should watch for specifically is anything involving real property outside Williamson County, since that can require an ancillary filing in the county where the property sits.

## Where Do You File Probate If You Live in Taylor?

Probate for a Taylor resident is generally filed in the Texas county where the deceased primarily resided at the time of death - for most Taylor residents, that's Williamson County, using the same probate court that serves Georgetown. The location of real property doesn't determine where probate is filed; the decedent's residence does.

Where it gets more complex: if the estate includes real property located in a different county (which happens more often for Taylor residents than for those living centrally in Williamson County), that property may require an additional filing in the county where it's located, even though the main probate case stays in the county of residence. This is exactly the kind of detail worth confirming with [a quick legal consultation](https://lilliearnold.legalshieldassociate.com/) early, rather than discovering it partway through.

## What Does Probate Cost for a Taylor Family?

Probate costs for a Taylor family handling a straightforward, uncontested estate typically run $1,500-$4,500 in flat attorney fees, or $250-$500+ per hour, plus court filing fees - the same general range that applies across Williamson County. Costs increase when the estate involves property or heirs in more than one county, since that can mean additional filings beyond the primary case.

What adds cost specifically for cross-county situations:

- An ancillary filing in a second county if real property is located there
- Additional time locating and confirming heirs if some live out of the immediate area
- Title work that has to account for property records held in a different county's system

None of this is unusual or especially difficult to handle - it just needs to be identified early rather than assumed away.

## Does Independent Administration Still Apply in Taylor?

Yes - independent administration is available to Taylor estates on the same terms as anywhere else in Texas: the will has to authorize it, or the heirs have to agree to request it, and from there the executor can manage the estate without seeking court approval for each individual step. Being near a county line doesn't change eligibility for this faster path.

This is worth emphasizing because families sometimes assume a more complicated-sounding situation (property near a county line, family spread across two counties) automatically means a more complicated legal process. It doesn't, as long as the will itself is in order.

## How Does This Compare to Other Nearby Areas?

The underlying probate framework for Taylor matches what applies in [Hutto & Williamson County](https://jredmonson.github.io/law/probate-attorney-hutto-tx/) and the broader [Georgetown probate guide](https://jredmonson.github.io/law/probate-attorney-georgetown-tx/) almost exactly - the differences are in the specific logistics of a given estate, not the legal rules themselves.

## FAQ

**Q: Do I file probate in Williamson County if I live in Taylor?**
A: In most cases, yes - probate is filed in the county where the deceased primarily resided, which for most Taylor residents is Williamson County. If the deceased actually resided in a different county at the time of death, that changes the filing location.

**Q: What if the estate includes property in a different county?**
A: The main probate case still stays in the county of residence, but real property located in a different county may require an additional ancillary filing there to properly transfer title. This is worth flagging to your attorney at the very start of the process.

**Q: Does living near a county line make probate more expensive?**
A: Not by itself. What raises cost is whether an ancillary filing is actually needed for out-of-county property, not proximity to a county line on its own. Many Taylor estates have no property outside Williamson County and proceed exactly like a Georgetown case would.

**Q: How do I know if my family's estate qualifies for independent administration?**
A: This depends primarily on whether the will authorizes it or whether all heirs are willing to agree to request it - not on where in Williamson County the deceased lived. A quick review of the will is usually enough to answer this question.
