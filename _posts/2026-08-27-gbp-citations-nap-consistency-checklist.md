## Start With the Data Aggregators, Not the Small Directories

The checklist should start with the handful of data aggregators - Data Axle, Neustar Localeze, and Foursquare among the most influential - because dozens of smaller directories pull their listing data directly from these sources rather than maintaining independent records. Correcting an aggregator record fixes the root of the problem; correcting only the smaller downstream directories individually means the wrong data can resurface the next time a site re-syncs from the aggregator.

Industry estimates suggest a meaningful share of local business directory listings online are populated or refreshed via aggregator feeds rather than direct business submission, which is exactly why starting upstream saves significant cleanup time compared to chasing corrections site by site.

## Check the Major General Directories Next

After the aggregators, the checklist moves to major general-purpose directories - Yelp, Apple Maps, Bing Places, the Better Business Bureau, and Facebook - since these carry independent weight in local search evaluation and are commonly the first alternate sources a customer checks after Google itself. Each of these should be checked directly rather than assumed correct, since they don't always pull from the same aggregator feeds as smaller sites.

**What to check on each major directory:**

- Business name matches the GBP exactly, including punctuation and suffixes (LLC, Inc., etc. included or omitted consistently)
- Full address matches exactly, including suite or unit number formatting (Suite 200 vs. Ste. 200 vs. #200 - pick one format and use it everywhere)
- Phone number is the current, active line - not a legacy number still forwarding or, worse, disconnected
- Website URL points to the current live site, not a retired domain or an old landing page

A mismatch in any one of these fields on a high-authority directory carries more weight than the same mismatch buried on a low-traffic niche site, so prioritizing these first is the efficient order to work through the list.

## Check Industry-Specific and Niche Directories

Industry-specific directories - contractor licensing databases, legal directories like Avvo, medical directories like Healthgrades, or local chamber of commerce listings - matter because they carry topical relevance signals beyond generic NAP consistency, confirming not just that a business exists but that it legitimately operates in its claimed category. These are easy to overlook because they're less visible than Yelp or Facebook, but they're often where the oldest, most neglected data actually lives.

A business that changed its name, merged with another company, or shifted its core service offering years ago frequently finds its most outdated information sitting in one of these smaller, industry-specific listings rather than on a major platform - simply because nobody thought to check it during the more visible parts of a rebrand.

## Watch for Duplicate Listings, Not Just Wrong Data

Duplicate listings on the same directory are a distinct problem from incorrect data and need their own check - they happen when a previous marketing vendor, a franchise transition, or an old business listing service created a second profile for the same location that was never merged or removed. A duplicate splits review counts, confuses which listing is authoritative, and can actively work against the primary, correctly maintained listing.

Searching the exact business name and address together, rather than just the business name alone, is the most reliable way to surface duplicates that a simple name search would otherwise miss, since duplicates are sometimes registered under a slightly varied name.

## Lock the Format Before Creating Anything New

Before creating any new citations to fill gaps, the checklist calls for locking a single, exact NAP format - the precise business name, full address formatting, and phone number presentation - in a shared reference document, so every future listing created by anyone on the team uses the identical format from the start rather than introducing a new variation. This single step prevents the next few years of drift that caused the original problem.

**A locked NAP reference should specify:**

- The exact legal or DBA business name as it should appear everywhere, with punctuation spelled out
- Full street address with a single, consistent suite/unit format
- One phone number, confirmed active, used on every listing without exception
- The canonical website URL, including whether it includes "www" and "https"

Once that document exists, new citation creation and any future directory submissions become simple copy-paste tasks instead of a fresh judgment call each time - and it's the same reference a business should hand to any future vendor, employee, or agency that touches its online listings.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many total citations does a typical local business actually need?**
Most local SEO guidance suggests 30-50 solid, relevant citations is a reasonable target for most small to mid-sized local businesses, prioritizing accuracy and relevance over raw volume - a handful of correct, high-authority citations outperforms dozens of low-quality or duplicate ones.

**Does a slight formatting difference, like "St." versus "Street," actually matter?**
Minor formatting variations matter less than factual mismatches like a wrong address or disconnected phone number, but picking one consistent format and using it everywhere removes any ambiguity and is a best practice worth following even for the smaller details.

**Can a business run this checklist itself without hiring anyone?**
Yes, all of these directories are searchable and most allow free listing claims and corrections directly, though doing it thoroughly across 20-30+ sources and monitoring for future drift is a real time investment most owners underestimate before setting it aside unfinished.

**What's the fastest directory to fix and see results from?**
Google Business Profile itself updates essentially immediately since it's self-managed, but for external citations, the major data aggregators tend to propagate corrections faster than small independent directories, which is another reason to start the cleanup there.
