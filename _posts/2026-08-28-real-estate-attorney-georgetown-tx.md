## What a Real Estate Attorney in Georgetown Actually Does

A real estate attorney reviews purchase contracts before you sign, checks title reports for liens or ownership defects, handles deed transfers (adding or removing a name from a property, transferring property into a trust), and represents clients in disputes over boundaries, easements, or contract breaches. In Texas, much of this work overlaps with what title companies handle as a matter of routine, but an attorney becomes essential the moment something falls outside the standard script.

For Georgetown specifically, that "something non-standard" often means new-construction contracts with builder-favorable terms that a standard title company review won't flag, or boundary questions on the area's larger, rural-transitioning lots where old fence lines don't match a modern survey.

## Do You Actually Need an Attorney for a Texas Closing?

Texas law doesn't require a licensed attorney to be present at every residential real estate closing - title companies routinely handle standard purchase and sale closings without one. An attorney becomes important specifically when the transaction includes something a title company isn't equipped to evaluate: a contract with unusual contingencies, a title defect that needs to be resolved before closing, a dispute between buyer and seller, or a transfer that involves a trust, an estate, or multiple owners with competing interests.

The practical rule of thumb: if your transaction is a standard purchase with a standard contract and a clean title, a title company closing is likely sufficient. If anything about the deal feels non-standard - the seller is a builder using their own contract, there's a title issue the report flagged, or you're transferring property as part of an estate or a divorce - that's when a real estate attorney's review earns its cost.

## Deed Transfers: A Common but Easy-to-Get-Wrong Task

Adding a family member to a deed, transferring property into a living trust, or removing a deceased owner's name are all common tasks that seem simple but have real legal consequences if done incorrectly - the wrong deed type can trigger unintended tax consequences, create title problems for a future sale, or fail to accomplish what the family actually intended. A quitclaim deed, for example, transfers whatever interest the grantor has (if any) with no warranty of clear title, which is very different from a warranty deed - using the wrong one for the situation is a common and avoidable mistake.

This is also a task that connects directly back to estate planning: property that's meant to pass to heirs or avoid probate often needs a specific kind of deed or needs to be properly titled in a trust, which is worth coordinating with your broader estate plan rather than treating as a one-off task. A transfer-on-death deed, for example, is a Texas-specific tool that lets a property pass directly to a named beneficiary outside of probate while the current owner retains full control during their lifetime - a genuinely useful option for a lot of Georgetown homeowners that a general-purpose will alone doesn't accomplish, and one that needs to be prepared and recorded correctly to actually work as intended.

## Title Issues: What Actually Delays a Closing

The most common title issues that delay a Williamson County closing are old liens that were never properly released, boundary discrepancies between an old survey and current property lines, and unresolved ownership questions from a prior inheritance that wasn't handled through a clean probate or deed transfer. Each of these can typically be resolved, but resolving them takes time - which is exactly why catching a title issue early, before a closing deadline is bearing down, matters so much.

## HOA Liens and Restrictive Covenants in Newer Georgetown Subdivisions

Many of Georgetown's newer subdivisions - and the newer construction throughout Williamson County generally - come with a homeowners association and a set of recorded restrictive covenants that go well beyond typical zoning rules, covering everything from fence height and exterior paint colors to whether you can park a boat in your driveway. An HOA's power isn't just aesthetic: unpaid dues can result in a lien against the property, and in some cases can lead to foreclosure if left unresolved, which makes HOA due-diligence during a purchase, and prompt attention to any dues dispute afterward, a genuinely important piece of the legal picture.

Buyers should specifically request and review the HOA's governing documents (covenants, bylaws, and current financial statements) before closing, not after - an attorney or a covered plan consultation can help interpret restrictions that read as boilerplate but carry real consequences, such as rental restrictions that would prevent using the property as an investment or short-term rental down the road.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do I need a real estate attorney to close on a house in Texas?**
**A:** Not always - Texas doesn't require an attorney at standard residential closings, and title companies routinely handle them. An attorney becomes important when something is non-standard: a title defect, an unusual contract, or a dispute between the parties.

**Q: What does a real estate attorney cost in Georgetown, TX?**
**A:** A standalone contract or closing document review typically costs $300-$1,000+ depending on complexity; a prepaid legal membership can include contract review as part of a flat monthly fee instead of a per-transaction charge.

**Q: What's the difference between a warranty deed and a quitclaim deed?**
**A:** A warranty deed guarantees the grantor has clear title and will defend against claims to the contrary, while a quitclaim deed only transfers whatever interest the grantor has, if any, with no such guarantee - using the wrong one for a given situation can create real problems later.

**Q: What commonly delays a real estate closing in Williamson County?**
**A:** Unreleased old liens, boundary discrepancies with a current survey, and unresolved ownership from a prior inheritance are among the most common issues - catching these early, before the closing deadline, gives more time to resolve them cleanly.

**Q: What is a transfer-on-death deed in Texas?**
**A:** A transfer-on-death deed lets a Texas property owner name a beneficiary who automatically receives the property when the owner dies, without going through probate, while the owner keeps full control and can revoke or change it during their lifetime - it must be properly prepared and recorded before death to be effective.
