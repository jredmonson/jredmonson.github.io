- A full-service agency should deliver architecture, security hardening, performance testing, hosting strategy, and ongoing maintenance as one package, not separate line items sold piecemeal.

A full-service WordPress website development agency is a different proposition once you move from a five-page brochure site to a platform carrying real traffic, customer data, or transactional load. Most agencies market themselves as full-service, but the term gets stretched to cover shops that do design and basic setup and call it a day. That works fine for a local plumber's site. It does not work for a corporate platform where a security gap, an unplanned traffic spike, or a poorly indexed database table can cost real money.

The failure modes are the tell. A small business site that goes down for twenty minutes is an inconvenience. A corporate platform that goes down during a product launch, a funding announcement, or a seasonal traffic surge is a business problem with a dollar figure attached. The same is true for security - a defaced brochure site is embarrassing; a breached platform holding customer records is a liability. If you're evaluating agencies for something at this scale, the vetting bar has to be higher than "do I like their portfolio."

## What "Full-Service" Should Actually Mean at This Scale

Full-service, for a corporate-scale WordPress platform, means the agency owns architecture, security, performance, hosting strategy, and maintenance under one accountable relationship - not a design shop that outsources the technical pieces and disclaims responsibility when something breaks. If any of these five areas is missing from the proposal, it's not full-service, regardless of what the sales page says.

Here's the baseline checklist a legitimate proposal should cover:

- **Architecture and planning** - custom database schema decisions, not a default theme install
- **Security configuration** - hardened beyond a security plugin's default settings
- **Performance engineering** - tested under simulated load, not just a PageSpeed screenshot
- **Hosting and infrastructure recommendation** - matched to actual traffic patterns
- **Maintenance and monitoring** - documented response times, not "we'll get to it"

Agencies that specialize in [custom corporate WordPress architectures](https://jredmonson.github.io/local-business/custom-corporate-wordpress-architectures/) tend to lead with this checklist unprompted. Agencies that don't will make you ask for each piece individually - and that's usually a sign they're pricing them as add-ons later.

## Custom Architecture: Why Default Themes Break Down

A corporate WordPress platform needs custom-built architecture because default themes and generic plugin stacks aren't designed for the data volume, user roles, or integration complexity that real business operations require. What works for a blog with a contact form does not work for a platform with thousands of records, multiple user permission tiers, and third-party API connections.

The common failure pattern looks like this:

| Small Business Site | Corporate-Scale Platform |
|---|---|
| Single admin user | Multiple role tiers with permission boundaries |
| A handful of static pages | Dynamic content pulled from custom database tables |
| One or two plugins | Integrated CRM, payment, and analytics connections |
| Shared hosting is fine | Traffic spikes require load-aware infrastructure |
| Design is the main deliverable | Design is one of five equally weighted deliverables |

When an agency skips custom architecture and just layers plugins onto a stock theme, the platform works fine in a demo and starts breaking once real users, real data, and real integrations hit it. This is the single most common reason corporate WordPress projects get rebuilt within two years - the original build wasn't architected for the load it eventually carried.

## Security Configuration: Beyond the Default Plugin

Security for a corporate WordPress platform has to go past installing a popular security plugin and calling it done - it requires server-level hardening, role-based access control, regular vulnerability scanning, and a documented incident response plan. A plugin catches known threats; it doesn't fix a misconfigured server or an overprivileged user account.

What a real security configuration includes:

- Server-level firewall rules, not just application-level filtering
- Two-factor authentication enforced for all admin-level accounts
- Role-based permissions so no account has more access than its job requires
- Scheduled vulnerability scanning with a documented patch response window
- File integrity monitoring to flag unauthorized changes
- A written incident response plan - who gets notified, and how fast

## Performance and Speed Under Real Load

Performance for a corporate platform has to be tested under simulated real-world traffic, not just measured with a single-page speed test on a quiet server. A site that loads fast with no visitors tells you nothing about how it behaves during a traffic spike, a product launch, or a seasonal surge.

The difference between a speed score and real performance:

- **Page speed test**: measures one page, one visitor, ideal conditions
- **Load testing**: simulates hundreds or thousands of concurrent users
- **Database query optimization**: prevents slowdowns as content volume grows
- **CDN and caching strategy**: reduces server load during traffic spikes
- **Image and asset optimization**: matters at scale, not just for scores

An agency focused on [speed-optimized WordPress development](https://jredmonson.github.io/local-business/speed-optimized-wordpress-development/) should be running load simulations before launch, not after something breaks. Speed at corporate scale isn't a vanity metric for Google rankings - it's a direct predictor of whether the platform holds up during the exact moments it matters most, like a marketing push or a press mention.

## Hosting, Uptime, and Infrastructure Decisions

Hosting for a corporate WordPress platform should be selected based on documented traffic patterns and growth projections, not defaulted to whatever hosting package the agency resells for margin. The right hosting tier depends on concurrent user load, database size, and how quickly traffic can spike - not a flat monthly price point.

Questions to ask before accepting a hosting recommendation:

1. What uptime SLA is guaranteed in writing, and what's the penalty if it's missed?
2. Is the hosting environment isolated (dedicated resources) or shared with other clients?
3. What's the backup frequency, and how fast is restoration tested - not just promised?
4. Who gets paged first if the site goes down at 2 a.m.?
5. Is there a staging environment for testing updates before they hit production?

An agency that can't answer all five clearly is not equipped to host a corporate-scale platform, even if their design work is excellent. Design and infrastructure are separate skill sets, and a lot of agencies are strong on one and weak on the other.

## Maintenance, Monitoring, and Response Time

Ongoing maintenance for a corporate platform needs a documented response-time commitment, active uptime monitoring, and a clear escalation path - not a vague "we'll fix issues as they come up" arrangement. Without a written SLA, there's no accountability when something breaks at an inconvenient time.

What a real maintenance agreement should specify:

| Element | What to Look For |
|---|---|
| Response time | Written commitment (e.g., 2-hour response for critical issues) |
| Monitoring | 24/7 automated uptime and error monitoring |
| Update cadence | Scheduled plugin/core updates, tested on staging first |
| Backup verification | Regular test restores, not just "backups run nightly" |
| Reporting | Monthly summary of uptime, incidents, and updates applied |

Maintenance is where a lot of agencies quietly reduce service after the contract is signed. Get the response-time and reporting commitments in writing during negotiation, not after the first incident when you're trying to hold them to a verbal promise.

## Cost Structure and What Corporate-Scale Really Runs

Cost for a genuinely full-service corporate WordPress build should reflect the complexity of architecture, security, and infrastructure work involved - not a flat rate borrowed from small business pricing. A brochure site and a platform handling user data or transactions require fundamentally different amounts of engineering time, and the price should show that.

Rough categories to expect (actual numbers vary by agency and scope, so treat this as a framework, not a quote):

- **Discovery and architecture planning**: fixed project fee, scoped before build begins
- **Custom development**: hourly or milestone-based, tied to feature complexity
- **Security hardening**: often bundled into build cost, sometimes a separate audit fee
- **Hosting and infrastructure**: recurring monthly cost, separate from the agency's dev fee
- **Maintenance retainer**: monthly fee tied to response-time SLA tier

If a proposal quotes one flat number for everything with no breakdown, ask for the itemized version. You want to see what you're paying for architecture versus hosting versus ongoing maintenance - bundled pricing with no visibility makes it hard to know if you're overpaying for one piece to subsidize another.

## How to Vet an Agency Before You Sign

Vetting a full-service WordPress agency for a corporate platform means asking for specific evidence - past load-testing reports, security audit samples, uptime SLA documents - rather than accepting a portfolio of nice-looking sites as proof of technical capability. Design portfolios show design skill; they don't show what happens under load or under attack.

Questions worth asking in the sales process:

- Can you show me a security audit or hardening checklist from a past project?
- What was your worst downtime incident with a client, and how did you handle it?
- Do you load-test before launch, and can I see a sample report?
- What's your average response time for a critical issue, in writing?
- Who specifically owns hosting and security - is it in-house or outsourced to a third party?

An agency confident in its technical delivery will answer these without hesitation. One that gets vague or redirects to design examples is telling you where its actual strengths lie.

## FAQ

**Q: How is a corporate WordPress build different from a small business website?**
A: A corporate build has to account for higher traffic volume, more complex user permissions, third-party integrations, and greater security risk. Small business sites can often run on default themes and shared hosting; corporate platforms typically need custom architecture, dedicated hosting resources, and documented security and uptime protocols.

**Q: What's a reasonable uptime SLA to expect?**
A: Most agencies serving corporate clients quote uptime commitments in the 99.9% range, though the specific number matters less than whether it's backed by a written penalty clause and monitored transparently. Ask for the SLA document, not just a verbal percentage.

**Q: Do I need custom development, or can plugins handle everything?**
A: Plugins can handle a lot, but stacking too many for core functionality increases security risk and slows the site down as traffic grows. For platforms with real data volume or custom workflows, purpose-built development is usually more stable long-term than a heavy plugin stack.

**Q: How often should security audits happen after launch?**
A: At minimum, quarterly vulnerability scans with immediate patching for anything critical. Platforms handling sensitive customer data or payment information often warrant more frequent review, especially after major plugin or core updates.
