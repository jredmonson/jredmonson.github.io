## Key Takeaways

- Early-stage LLCs and startups get the most value from legal review on foundational documents - operating agreements, founder agreements, first client contracts.
- A flat monthly fee removes the friction that normally causes early-stage businesses to skip legal review on smaller documents.
- Mistakes in foundational agreements compound as a business grows, making early review disproportionately valuable compared to the same review a few years later.

Most LLCs and startups run for years on foundational documents nobody ever had a lawyer actually review - an operating agreement pulled from a template, a founder agreement handled with a handshake, a first client contract signed as-is. ## Why Foundational Documents Matter More Than They Seem

The documents an LLC signs in its first year - the operating agreement, founder agreements if there's more than one owner, and the earliest client and vendor contracts - tend to become the template for everything that follows. A gap or ambiguity in an early operating agreement doesn't just affect that one document; it becomes the pattern the business operates under for years, often until a dispute forces a closer look. Reviewing these documents while they're still easy to change is meaningfully cheaper than fixing them after a problem surfaces.

## What Ongoing Coverage Actually Looks Like for a Startup

Once a plan is active, the practical benefit is having somewhere to send a document before signing it, without weighing whether the review is "worth" an hourly bill. A vendor contract, a new hire's offer letter, a terms-of-service page - all of these get looked at before they go out, not just when something goes wrong with one of them later. That shift, from reactive to proactive review, is where most of the real value shows up over time.

## What This Doesn't Replace

A flat-fee plan is well-suited to recurring document review and general compliance questions, but it isn't a substitute for specialized counsel on more complex matters like a funding round, a merger, or significant litigation - those situations typically call for a dedicated attorney with specific transactional experience. Knowing that boundary upfront avoids the wrong expectation later.

## Is This Worth It for an Early-Stage Business?

For an LLC or startup that currently operates with zero legal review on its documents - which describes a large share of small businesses in their first few years - the answer tends to be yes, specifically because the alternative isn't "no risk," it's "unreviewed risk that hasn't surfaced yet." **Should a brand-new LLC get a legal plan before it has any revenue?**

**Does this cover a funding round or investor agreement?**
Complex transactional matters like funding rounds generally fall outside standard coverage and typically call for dedicated specialized counsel.

**Can more than one founder use the same plan?**
