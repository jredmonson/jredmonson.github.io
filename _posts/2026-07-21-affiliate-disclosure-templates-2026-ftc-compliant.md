> **Quick Answer:** An FTC-compliant affiliate disclosure needs to say, in plain language, that you earn a commission if the reader buys through your link, placed *before* the link itself (not buried in a footer or about page), using words a reasonable person understands without clicking anything else.

## Key Takeaways

- The FTC requires disclosures to be "clear and conspicuous" — placement matters as much as wording.
- A disclosure buried at the bottom of a page or only in an "About" section does not meet FTC guidance.
- Vague phrases like "this post may contain affiliate links" without explaining what that means often fall short.
- The FTC updated its Endorsement Guides in 2023, and enforcement has increased since — 2026 is not the year to wing this.
- A reusable template saves time across every post, email, and video description without reinventing the wording each time.

The fix isn't complicated. It's not a lawyer-drafted contract. It's a specific placement pattern and specific wording pulled from FTC guidance rather than from whatever ranked on page one of Google. ## What the FTC Actually Requires

The FTC Endorsement Guides (last revised in 2023) don't hand you exact wording. They hand you a standard: disclosures must be clear and conspicuous, meaning an average reader notices and understands them without extra effort. That standard gets interpreted through FTC enforcement actions and guidance documents, and a few patterns show up consistently.

Three things the FTC has flagged as insufficient in guidance and enforcement:

- A disclosure only on a separate "disclosure" or "about" page, disconnected from the actual affiliate link
- Disclosure language buried below the fold or after the call-to-action
- Ambiguous wording like "sponsored" or "partner link" with no explanation of what that means financially

| FTC Requirement | What Fails | What Passes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear language | "Partner content" | "I earn a commission if you buy through this link" |
| Proximity to link | Disclosure only in footer | Disclosure directly above or beside the link |
| Platform-specific placement | One disclosure for blog + video + email | Separate disclosure per platform, each visible before the CTA |
| Understandable to average reader | Legal jargon, hyperlinked disclaimers | Plain sentence, no click required to read it |

None of this requires a lawyer. It requires reading the actual guidance once and building a template around it instead of around whatever generic line is circulating on marketing forums.

## Where Placement Actually Goes Wrong

Most affiliates get the wording roughly right and the placement completely wrong. This is the part that costs people, because a technically accurate sentence placed in the wrong spot still fails the "conspicuous" test.

The FTC's own guidance is specific that disclosures need to appear *before* the reader encounters the affiliate link or claim, not after they've already clicked or scrolled past. A disclosure at the very bottom of a 2,000-word review, after the reader has already clicked three affiliate links, doesn't do its job even if the wording is perfect.

Here's where disclosures commonly need to live, by format:

- **Blog posts**: near the top, before the first affiliate link, and again near any major CTA
- **YouTube videos**: verbally stated in the video AND in the description's first few lines (not buried under timestamps)
- **Email newsletters**: before the first affiliate link in the body, not just in a footer legal block
- **Social media captions**: in the caption itself, not only as a hashtag like #ad buried after ten other hashtags
- **Podcast**: spoken disclosure near the mention, not just in show notes

A single disclosure line copy-pasted into a footer template covers none of these placement requirements individually. That's the gap that generic templates from 2019-era blog posts never accounted for.

## Wording That Actually Holds Up

Generic wording fails because it's vague on purpose — vague enough to sound legal-ish without committing to anything specific. Real FTC-aligned wording says three things directly: that a link is affiliate, that you earn money from it, and that it costs the reader nothing extra.

Compare these two approaches:

| Generic (Common, Weak) | FTC-Aligned (Specific) |
|---|---|
| "This post may contain affiliate links." | "I earn a commission if you buy through the link below, at no extra cost to you." |
| "Sponsored post." | "This company paid me to try their product and share my honest opinion." |
| "Partner content." | "I'm an affiliate for [Company] and earn a commission on sales made through my link." |

The left column technically discloses something. The right column discloses the thing the FTC actually cares about: the financial relationship and its effect on the reader's cost. "May contain" is a hedge word that shouldn't be in a disclosure at all — either the post contains affiliate links or it doesn't.

## Building a Reusable Template System

Once the wording and placement rules are clear, the actual work is turning them into a repeatable template you drop into every post, video, and email without rewriting it each time. This is where most affiliates either waste time reinventing the wheel per post or under-disclose because writing a new disclosure every time feels like friction.

A workable template system needs:

1. One core disclosure sentence that's platform-agnostic
2. Platform-specific variants (blog, video, email, social) built from that core sentence
3. A placement rule attached to each variant ("top of post," "first 3 lines of description," etc.)
4. A version for paid partnerships separate from standard affiliate links, since FTC treats material connections broadly

Building this once and reusing it across every piece of content is the difference between compliance being a recurring headache and compliance being a five-second copy-paste. That's the entire value of having actual templates instead of writing disclosures from memory each time.

## Common Mistakes That Still Show Up in 2026

Even with more awareness of FTC rules than five years ago, the same handful of mistakes keep showing up across blogs, YouTube channels, and Instagram accounts. None of these are exotic — they're the boring, easy-to-fix ones that people skip because disclosure feels like an afterthought.

- Using "#ad" or "#sponsored" as the only disclosure, buried among a dozen other hashtags
- Disclosing on the homepage but not on the individual post with the actual affiliate link
- Assuming a disclosure policy page linked in the nav bar counts as disclosure on every page
- Using disclosure language that's technically true but written in legal density nobody actually reads
- Forgetting that disclosure requirements apply to unpaid affiliate relationships too, not just paid sponsorships

That last one trips up more people than expected. The FTC's standard isn't "did you get paid upfront" — it's whether there's a material connection that could affect how a reasonable person views your recommendation. A commission on a sale counts, whether or not any money changed hands before the review was written.

## FAQ

**Q: Do I need a different affiliate disclosure for every social media platform?**
A: Yes, generally. The FTC evaluates disclosure conspicuousness in the context of each platform's format, so a disclosure that works in a blog post footer won't satisfy requirements on Instagram or TikTok, where it needs to be in the caption or on-screen, not just linked elsewhere.

**Q: Is "this post contains affiliate links" enough on its own?**
A: It's a start but often incomplete. The FTC's guidance favors language that also explains you earn a commission and that it costs the reader nothing extra — the phrase alone doesn't spell out the financial relationship clearly enough to fully meet the standard.

**Q: Where exactly should the disclosure go on a blog post?**
A: Before the first affiliate link appears, ideally near the top of the post, not just in the footer or a separate disclosure page. If there are multiple major affiliate links or CTAs throughout, repeating the disclosure near each one is safer.

**Q: Does this apply if I only get free products, not commissions?**
A: Yes. FTC guidance covers any material connection that could influence how a reasonable person views your content, which includes free products, gifted items, and discounts, not just cash commissions.
