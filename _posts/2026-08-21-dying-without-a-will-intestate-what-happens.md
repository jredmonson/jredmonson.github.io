## Key Takeaways

- Dying without a will means a state's intestate succession laws - not personal wishes - determine who inherits, which rarely matches what someone would have actually chosen.
- The process typically takes longer and costs more than settling an estate with a valid will already in place.
- Only 26% of Americans currently have a will, according to Trust & Will's 2026 Estate Planning Report - meaning most estates today are at real risk of going through this process.

"Intestate" is the legal term for dying without a valid will, and it triggers a specific, state-determined process that most people have never actually looked into until it directly affects their family. As covered in the [Make-A-Will Month guide](https://jredmonson.github.io/law/national-make-a-will-month-guide/), this is exactly the gap National Make-A-Will Month exists to close.

## How Intestate Succession Actually Works

Every state has its own intestate succession laws - a default, legally mandated order of who inherits an estate when there's no valid will. These laws follow a fairly rigid formula based on family relationships (spouse, children, parents, siblings, in that general order), with no ability to account for personal wishes, specific relationships, or circumstances a will would normally address, like an unmarried partner, a stepchild, or a friend someone wanted to include.

## Who Actually Gets Left Out

The people most likely to be left out entirely under intestate succession are exactly the people a will is often written specifically to protect: unmarried partners, stepchildren who were never legally adopted, and close friends or extended family members outside the legally recognized inheritance order. This gap matters more than it might seem - the 2026 Estate Planning Report found that 68% of unmarried couples in serious relationships currently have no estate documents at all, meaning a large share of long-term partners have zero legal claim to each other's estate without one.

## The Time and Cost Difference

An estate without a will typically takes longer to settle through probate court, since the court has to establish heirs and resolve the estate according to state formula rather than following a document that already answers those questions. That extra time commonly comes with added legal and administrative costs - the exact costs a will is designed to help avoid in the first place. Estates with a clear, valid will in place generally move through probate more predictably and with fewer court-required steps.

## What This Means If You Have Minor Children

Intestate succession doesn't address guardianship - if both parents die without a will, a court decides who raises minor children, without any documented parental preference to guide that decision. This is one of the most consequential gaps in dying without a will, and one of the clearest reasons a will matters regardless of the size of an estate.

## Closing This Gap

The fix for all of this is the same regardless of estate size or family complexity: a legally valid will that names an executor, specifies beneficiaries, and - if applicable - names a guardian for minor children. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a spouse automatically inherit everything if there's no will?**
Not necessarily - most states split an intestate estate between a surviving spouse and children according to a set formula, which can mean a spouse doesn't inherit the full estate even when that would have been the deceased's actual wish.

**Can an unmarried partner inherit anything under intestate succession?**
Generally no - unmarried partners typically have no legal standing under intestate succession laws, regardless of relationship length, unless a will specifically names them.

**Is intestate succession the same in every state?**
No - specific rules and inheritance order vary by state, which is part of why the outcome can be genuinely unpredictable without a will already in place.
