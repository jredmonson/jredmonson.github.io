## What a Family Law Attorney in Georgetown Actually Handles

Family law covers divorce, child custody and visitation (called "conservatorship" and "possession" under Texas law), child support, spousal support, and modifications to existing orders when circumstances change. A Georgetown-based attorney working regularly in Williamson County family courts will know the local judges' general tendencies and the court's typical scheduling timeline, which can meaningfully affect how a case unfolds.

Most family law matters start with an initial consultation to understand the situation - whether the case is likely to be contested or can be resolved through agreement, whether children are involved, and what the financial picture looks like. That initial framing shapes almost everything that follows, including cost, because an uncontested case resolved by agreement is a fundamentally different (and less expensive) process than a contested case that goes to trial.

## Custody, Conservatorship, and What Texas Courts Actually Look At

Texas uses the term "conservatorship" rather than "custody," and courts generally start from a presumption that both parents should be named joint managing conservators unless there's a specific reason not to - meaning shared decision-making authority, even if one parent has primary physical custody. Courts weigh the child's best interest using factors like each parent's involvement, stability, and ability to co-parent, not simply which parent asks first or which parent earns more.

A lot of the conflict in custody cases comes from parents assuming the legal standard matches what feels emotionally fair to them, when Texas law is actually built around a fairly specific, fact-driven best-interest framework. Getting a clear, early explanation of how that framework applies to your specific situation - before emotions and assumptions harden into a dispute - is one of the more valuable things an attorney consultation does early in a case.

## Divorce: Contested vs. Uncontested, and Why That Distinction Drives Cost

An uncontested divorce, where both spouses agree on the major terms (property division, custody, support), can often be finalized relatively quickly and at a fraction of the cost of a contested case that requires discovery, hearings, and possibly a trial. The single biggest cost lever in a divorce isn't the attorney's hourly rate - it's how much the two sides actually agree on going in.

That's also where early legal guidance pays for itself: understanding what a fair, realistic settlement looks like under Texas law - rather than guessing, or anchoring on what a friend's divorce looked like in a different state - can be the difference between resolving a case by agreement and ending up in a drawn-out contested fight over terms that a court would likely have ordered anyway.

## Modifications and Enforcement: When an Existing Order Needs to Change

Family law doesn't end when a divorce is finalized or a custody order is entered - life keeps changing, and Texas law allows either parent to request a modification when there's been a "material and substantial change in circumstances," such as a job relocation, a significant change in income, or a shift in a child's needs as they get older. Enforcement is a separate but related issue: when one parent isn't following an existing order (missed support payments, denied visitation), the other parent can go back to court to have the order enforced.

Both modifications and enforcement actions go through the same Williamson County family court that handled the original case, and having the original order and a clear record of what's changed - dates, communications, missed payments - makes a meaningful difference in how quickly a court can act. This is another area where an early consultation is valuable: understanding whether your situation actually meets the "material and substantial change" standard before filing can save time and expense on a modification request that a court would be unlikely to grant.

## Mediation as a Lower-Cost Alternative to a Contested Trial

Texas courts frequently require or strongly encourage mediation before a contested family law case goes to trial, and for good reason - a mediated settlement is typically faster and dramatically less expensive than litigating every issue in front of a judge. Mediation involves both parties and their attorneys working with a neutral third-party mediator to reach an agreement on custody, support, and property division, with a judge simply approving the final agreement rather than deciding the case from scratch.

For Georgetown-area families, mediation is worth raising early with an attorney, not treating as a last resort after months of contested litigation. Going in with realistic expectations - informed by an early consultation about what a Williamson County court would likely order anyway - tends to produce mediated agreements that both sides can actually live with, rather than a compromise reached purely out of litigation fatigue.
